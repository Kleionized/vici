This is the full first Vici course. The twelve weeks are a curriculum structure, not a claim that the brain or reward system biologically “resets” after 84 or 90 days. The course distinguishes problematic pornography use from normal sexuality. Shame, guilt, or moral disagreement alone are not treated as proof of addiction.

[Heading1] Week 1: Reset
[Heading2] Lesson 1: Surviving the Night
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“A journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step.” – Lao Tzu
[Heading3] 2. Tonight
You do not need to fix your life tonight.
Maybe you opened this after a relapse. Maybe the last few days have been bad. Maybe nothing dramatic happened at all. You are simply tired of ending up in the same place.
Good. That gives us somewhere to start.
For tonight, one decision is enough.
[Heading3] 3. Make the problem smaller
The mind likes to turn quitting into an enormous promise.
Never again. Ninety days. The rest of your life.
At midnight, those promises are almost useless. They ask a tired version of you to carry a future that has not happened yet.
So do not carry it.
Get to tomorrow.
[Heading3] 4. What tonight requires
For the next few hours, the job is simple: do not relapse.
Not forever.
Not for the next three months.
Not even for the rest of the week.
Just tonight.
Tomorrow, we can work on the habit itself. Tonight, make sure it does not get another round.
[Heading3] 5. Think about the last night
Go back to the last time it happened at night.
Where were you before the searching started?
Maybe you were in bed with your phone. Maybe you could not sleep. Maybe you had a bad day and wanted something that would switch your head off for a while.
There is usually an earlier moment when stopping is cheap. Before the search. Before the tab. Before you are fully caught in it.
Where do most of your night-time relapses begin?
In bed with my phone
Alone on my computer
When I can't sleep
Somewhere else
[Heading3] 6. Add distance
An urge at midnight is much more convincing when the whole habit sits one thumb-movement away.
Change that.
If the phone is across the room, you have to stand up. If it is downstairs, you have to leave the bed. If the laptop is closed and put away, you have to make another decision before anything happens.
That small gap matters.
Do not spend willpower where distance will do the job.
[Heading3] 7. Before you sleep
Once you have done that, you are finished for today.
No autopsy of the past. No huge promise about the future. No test of whether you are “strong enough.”
Get through tonight.
Tomorrow can have tomorrow.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“Well begun is half done.” – Aristotle
[Heading3] 8. Tonight's task
[TaskSummary] Put the device you use for porn out of reach before you sleep.
Set up tonight before you get tired. Use the option that matches where you sleep.
• Own bedroom: Set the alarm now. Charge the phone outside the room. Put a laptop or tablet in a closed bag, drawer, or cupboard away from the bed.
• Shared room: Put the device in a bag, locker, desk drawer, or fixed charging spot that you cannot reach while lying down.
• Studio, sofa bed, or temporary space: Put the device at the farthest practical point from where you sleep: a kitchen counter, shelf, zipped bag, or other fixed place.
• Phone needed as an alarm: Set the alarm first. Put the phone across the room or outside it. Turn off non-essential notifications before you put it down.
The task is done when you cannot reach the device you usually use for porn from bed without standing up and moving.
[Heading2] Lesson 2: A New Start
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“Begin at once to live, and count each separate day as a separate life.” – Seneca
[Heading3] 2. Look around
Your room may still be arranged for the life you are trying to leave.
Look at it.
Rubbish you stopped seeing. Clothes on the floor. Glasses by the bed. Tabs that have been open for weeks. Old material you kept “just in case.” Little routines that make the same evening easy to repeat.
Do not begin with an identity speech.
Begin with the room.
[Heading3] 3. Clear the obvious
Set aside the complicated questions.
Throw away the rubbish. Put the clothes away. Clear the desk. Take the glasses downstairs. Change the sheets if they need changing.
Then remove what you have kept because it leads you back to porn.
Saved images. Downloads. Accounts. Hidden folders. Physical material. Links you know by heart.
You do not need a clinical definition of “trigger” to delete something whose purpose you already understand.
If you know why it is there, you know.
[Heading3] 4. The room makes some choices easier
Your surroundings do not control you. They do keep offering suggestions.
A phone beside the pillow makes one set of actions easy. A laptop already open on the bed makes another. A room that has not changed in months can put you back into an old evening before you have thought much about it.
Cleaning the room will not cure the habit.
That is not the claim.
It removes some of the things that used to make porn easy to reach: the phone beside the pillow, the open laptop, the saved folder, and the account already logged in.
[Heading3] 5. Clear the digital room too
Your phone and laptop count as part of the environment.
Where are the easiest routes back for you? Select all that apply.
Saved photos, videos, or links
Social media accounts or feeds
Certain websites
Hidden folders or downloads
Physical material
Something else
Delete the obvious material. Unfollow the accounts you keep for one reason. Remove shortcuts, bookmarks, saved links, and easy routes you know you use.
You do not need to make the internet unusable.
Stop leaving yourself a door already open.
[Heading3] 6. Make the change visible
When the room is clean enough, change one thing on purpose.
Fresh sheets. A clear desk. A photograph. A book you actually want to read. Your guitar on a stand. A notebook where the old clutter was. Open the curtains.
Nothing dramatic.
It should simply look different enough that you notice.
If the room looks exactly the same, it is easy to fall back into the same evening. Change one visible thing so you notice the difference when you come back.
[Heading3] 7. Let identity follow
People often wait to feel like a different person before they act like one.
It usually happens the other way round.
You make the bed, delete the folder, and leave the phone outside. Repeat those choices often enough and lying in the same room with the same device starts to feel less automatic.
Identity follows the evidence.
Give it some.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“First say to yourself what you would be; and then do what you have to do.” – Epictetus
[Heading3] 8. Today's task
[TaskSummary] Clean the space you use most and remove easy porn cues.
Set a 20-minute timer. Clean only the physical and digital space you control. Stop when the timer rings.
• Own room: Make the bed. Open the curtains or blinds. Put dirty clothes in a hamper or bag. Throw out rubbish. Clear the floor, desk, and bedside surface.
• Shared room: Clean your bed, desk, shelf, drawer, bag, and devices. Leave the other person's things alone.
• No fixed room: Pick one area you use every day - a desk, shelf, bag, bedside corner, or your side of a shared space - and clean that.
• Digital: Delete saved porn. Close old tabs. Log out of accounts you use to find it. Remove bookmarks and shortcuts. Move risky apps off the home screen.
The task is done when the timer rings and the area you use most is cleaner, with fewer porn-related files, accounts, or objects within easy reach.
[Heading2] Lesson 3: Get Outside
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“An early-morning walk is a blessing for the whole day.” – Henry David Thoreau
[Heading3] 2. Habits have places
Relapses rarely happen anywhere.
They tend to have addresses.
The bedroom. The bathroom. An empty house. A desk late at night. Somewhere private, familiar, and unlikely to be interrupted.
Repeat a habit in the same place often enough and the place becomes part of the sequence.
You enter. Close the door. Lie down. Pick up the phone.
After enough repetitions, reaching for the phone can begin before you have consciously decided to watch porn.
[Heading3] 3. Leave earlier
One of the simplest ways to interrupt that sequence is to leave.
Not after forty minutes of arguing with the urge.
Not after you have started searching.
Earlier.
If you catch yourself lying around bored, scrolling without a reason, getting restless, or slipping into the posture that usually comes before a relapse, stand up and go somewhere else.
Do not wait for the argument to get harder.
[Heading3] 4. Why outside works
Outside gives your attention work to do.
You move. Cross roads. Notice people. Feel the weather. Hear traffic. Decide where you are going. Watch where you step.
You are also less private.
Going outside changes what you are doing. You stop lying in the same room with the same screen in your hand. You start walking, looking around, crossing roads, and being around other people. That makes it harder to continue the exact sequence that usually leads to porn.
Sometimes that is all you need.
[Heading3] 5. Know your place
Where are you most likely to relapse?
My bedroom
The bathroom
Somewhere else at home
It isn't tied to one place
If one place stands out, remember it.
You are not banning yourself from the room.
You are learning not to stay there for an hour when you can already feel the routine beginning.
[Heading3] 6. Ignore the negotiation
Going outside can feel absurdly difficult when you are low.
Your mind starts bargaining.
Later. It's cold. I'm tired. There is nowhere to go. Ten more minutes.
You do not need somewhere impressive to go.
Walk around the block. Buy a drink. Sit on a bench. Get coffee. Go nowhere in particular.
The destination is not the point.
Leaving is.
[Heading3] 7. Keep the exit in mind
Later, you will learn several ways to handle an urge while it is happening.
The first one needs no technique.
If the room is making the urge worse, leave the room.
Solve the next problem somewhere else.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“All truly great thoughts are conceived while walking.” – Friedrich Nietzsche
[Heading3] 8. Today's task
[TaskSummary] Spend 20 minutes away from your usual relapse setting.
Spend 20 minutes away from the place where you are most likely to relapse.
• Good weather: Walk around the block, sit in a park, get a drink, run an errand, or take a route that keeps you moving.
• Bad weather: Use a library, cafe, gym, lobby, shopping centre, covered walkway, or another shared indoor place.
• Cannot leave home: Move to the least isolated part of the home. Open the curtains. Sit near other people if possible. Use a window, balcony, or doorway for fresh air.
• Urge already strong: Close the tab or app first. Leave the room before deciding anything else. Take the phone only if you need it for safety or directions.
The task is done when you have spent 20 minutes away from the usual relapse setting without continuing the same sexual or endless scrolling elsewhere.
[Heading2] Lesson 4: Fix Your Sleep
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“There is a time for many words, and there is also a time for sleep.” – Homer
[Heading3] 2. Two in the afternoon is not two in the morning
At 2 p.m., avoiding porn can feel obvious.
At 2 a.m., after three hours of scrolling, alone and tired, the same decision somehow becomes a debate.
Your values did not change.
The hour did.
So did your judgment.
[Heading3] 3. The empty part of the night
Irregular sleep creates long stretches in which almost nothing is happening.
You will sleep soon.
Then it is midnight.
Then one.
Then two.
You switch apps, lie around, pick the phone back up, put it down, pick it up again. By then you may already feel annoyed that you are still awake.
Those hours give the habit time and privacy.
A clearer end to the day removes some of them.
[Heading3] 4. Start with waking
Do not try to force yourself unconscious at exactly 11:00 every night.
Sleep does not work well as a command.
Start with the part you control more directly: when you get up.
Getting up at roughly the same time gives your sleep schedule one fixed point. After several days, you are also more likely to feel sleepy at a similar time at night.
You do not need military precision.
Aim for roughly the same time most days.
[Heading3] 5. Pick the time you can actually keep
Choose for the person who exists now.
Not the imaginary version of you who wakes at 5:30, runs ten kilometres, meditates for half an hour, cooks breakfast, and greets sunrise with perfect discipline.
You.
What time will you aim to wake up most days?
Before 7:00
7:00 to 8:00
8:00 to 9:00
After 9:00
Pick a time you could still respect on a mediocre morning.
[Heading3] 6. Make the last hour duller
You do not need a twelve-step night routine.
You need the day to stop extending itself.
Move the phone away from the bed. Lower the lights. Brush your teeth. Put on sleep clothes. Read. Listen to something quiet. Stop opening new things.
Let the night become boring.
Boring is useful here.
[Heading3] 7. Give the day an ending
You are not trying to force perfect sleep. You are trying to remove the long, tired stretch in bed when you usually keep scrolling.
The day should have a clear stopping point.
When the day ends clearly, you spend fewer tired hours asking yourself the same question over and over.
Close the day before the day starts making decisions for you.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“Sleep that knits up the ravelled sleave of care.” – William Shakespeare
[Heading3] 8. Today's task
[TaskSummary] Set tomorrow’s alarm and decide when and where your phone goes away tonight.
Set tomorrow's alarm now. Then choose the exact time and place where the phone goes away tonight.
• Normal school or work hours: Pick a realistic wake time. Put the phone away 30-60 minutes before you intend to sleep.
• Late shift or irregular hours: Use the same sequence around your actual schedule: finish work, wind down, phone away, sleep, then wake when your schedule requires.
• Phone is your alarm: Set the alarm before the cutoff. Put the phone across the room or outside the bedroom.
• Shared room: Use a bag, drawer, locker, or fixed charging spot away from the bed so the device has the same place every night.
The task is done when the alarm is set, the phone cutoff has a clock time, and the phone has a fixed place for the night.
[Heading2] Lesson 5: What Replaces Porn?
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“No great thing is created suddenly.” – Epictetus
[Heading3] 2. You took something out
The first few days have mostly been subtraction.
Less access. Less time alone in the old setting. Fewer late hours. Less porn.
That leaves a question people often forget to ask.
What will you do with the time that used to disappear into porn?
[Heading3] 3. Porn occupied more than the viewing
A habit takes more time than the minutes on the screen.
There is anticipation. Searching. Scrolling. Recovering afterwards. Thinking about doing it. Waiting until you can be alone. Reaching for it whenever nothing else feels interesting enough.
Take all of that away and the day can suddenly feel empty.
Empty time is not dangerous by itself.
But if the old habit is still the easiest answer to boredom, you have left it a job.
[Heading3] 4. The replacement does not need to improve your CV
You are not required to turn recovery into a productivity contest.
Play football. Pick up the guitar. Cook. Game with friends. Run. Draw. Read. Box. Code. Take photographs. Build something. Go somewhere.
Choose something you can enter, not merely consume.
You need something you can actually start when you would otherwise lie around scrolling: a book, a guitar, a workout, a recipe, a game with friends, a course, or a small project.
[Heading3] 5. Pick something that asks something of you
The strongest alternatives usually require participation.
Your hands move. Your body works. You make decisions. Practise. Learn. Talk. Build. Miss. Try again.
Compare that with lying in bed switching from TikTok to YouTube to Instagram until an hour disappears.
Both fill time.
The second kind of activity leaves you with something you did, learnt, made, or practised when the hour is over.
[Heading3] 6. Pick a direction
What would you most like to put more time into?
Exercise or sport
A creative hobby
Learning or building something
Something social
Something else
Do not search for your life purpose.
Pick something you already care about a little.
That is enough to begin.
[Heading3] 7. Build an answer before you need it
When an urge arrives later, you do not want your only question to be:
“How do I stop myself?”
Build another one.
“What else could I be doing right now?”
That question becomes useful once you have real answers.
Start collecting them.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“No man is free who is not master of himself.” – Epictetus
[Heading3] 8. Today's task
[TaskSummary] Spend 20 minutes learning something new.
Set a 20-minute timer and learn one new thing. Pick the topic within two minutes, then start.
• Computers: Follow one short coding tutorial, learn one command, build one tiny component, or understand how one tool works.
• Practical skill: Learn a Rubik's Cube algorithm, guitar chord, cooking technique, knot, repair, card trick, or exercise movement.
• Ideas: Read about one historical event, scientific idea, philosophical argument, language rule, or question you have wondered about.
• No idea what to choose: Open a book, course, or tutorial you already own and learn the next small section. Do not spend the session searching for the perfect topic.
The task is done when the 20 minutes ends and you can explain, demonstrate, or use one thing you could not before the session.
[Heading2] Lesson 6: Isolation
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“Man is by nature a social animal.” – Aristotle
[Heading3] 2. Look at the usual scene
Porn rarely happens halfway through dinner with friends.
You are usually not talking to someone. Training with someone. Sitting across a table from someone who knows you are there.
You are alone.
Often for longer than you meant to be.
That does not make solitude bad. It does make isolation worth noticing.
[Heading3] 3. Isolation arrives quietly
A difficult period can shrink your world without announcing itself.
You stay home once. Stop replying. Skip something you were invited to. Say you are tired. Eat alone. Spend another evening in your room.
None of those decisions looks serious on its own.
Then a week passes and you have barely had a real conversation.
Catch the sequence early: staying home, replying less, skipping plans, eating alone, then spending more and more of the evening by yourself.
[Heading3] 4. Contact is enough
You do not need to confess your whole problem to somebody today.
Have lunch with a friend. Call home. Go to training. Study beside someone. Send a message. Ask somebody for coffee. Stay after class for the conversation you would normally cut short.
Connection can be ordinary.
That is part of why it works.
You are looking for contact, not a dramatic disclosure.
[Heading3] 5. Who is already there?
Who could you have a little more contact with today?
A close friend
Family
Someone from work, school, or university
Someone I haven't spoken to recently
Someone else
Pick one person.
You do not need a reason cleverer than: “Want to grab food?”
[Heading3] 6. Do not disappear on the bad days
The day you most want to disappear into your room is also the day when a short call, meal with someone, or hour in a shared place can stop the whole evening becoming isolated.
A rough day makes isolation appealing because nobody asks anything of you there.
But too much isolation gives you long stretches of privacy, boredom, and uninterrupted screen time. Those are the hours in which porn is easiest to return to.
You do not need company every hour.
Just do not vanish.
[Heading3] 7. Keep one line open
There will be time later to talk about loneliness, intimacy, friendship, and relationships.
Today is smaller.
Keep one connection alive.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“What brings no benefit to the hive brings none to the bee.” – Marcus Aurelius
[Heading3] 8. Today's task
[TaskSummary] Spend at least 10 minutes talking with or being around another person.
Spend at least 10 minutes talking with or spending time with another person today. You do not need to mention recovery.
• Someone nearby: Eat with them, walk with them, sit in a common area, attend a class or club, or stay for a conversation instead of leaving immediately.
• People far away: Call someone. If you text, ask a question, suggest a call, or make a plan that gives them something to answer.
• No close person comes to mind: Go to a repeated social setting such as a sports session, class, club, study space, workplace common area, or regular cafe.
• Busy day: Use a short window: eat lunch with someone, walk with a colleague, or make a 10-minute call.
The task is done when you have spent at least 10 minutes talking with someone or doing something together.
[Heading2] Lesson 7: One Week In
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste much of it.” – Seneca
[Heading3] 2. Seven days
A week is not proof that you are cured.
It is not a magical threshold.
It is seven days in which you started changing the conditions around a habit that used to meet very little resistance.
Those seven days gave you evidence about which changes actually helped and which ones did not.
[Heading3] 3. Look at the week, not just the streak
On the first night, you only had to get through the evening without watching.
Since then you have looked at the room, removed some easy routes back, gone outside, put more structure around sleep, chosen somewhere else for your energy, and made contact with another person.
Some of it probably helped.
Some of it may have felt pointless.
Good. Now you have information.
[Heading3] 4. What helped?
Which change helped you most this week?
Keeping my phone away at night
Clearing my environment
Going outside
Improving my sleep
Finding something else to do
Spending time with someone
There is no correct answer.
There is no single routine that works for everyone. Pay attention to which changes helped you most: moving the phone, cleaning the room, getting outside, sleeping earlier, finding something to do, or spending time with other people.
Pay attention to that.
[Heading3] 5. What still caught you?
Now look at the harder parts.
Were nights still rough? Did you keep reaching for the phone without thinking? Did leaving the room help only after the urge had already become strong? Did the week feel almost the same as the one before?
Do not grade yourself.
Look for the specific thing that kept repeating: the same hour, room, device, feeling, or sequence before the urge.
The next few weeks will help you notice the specific hours, places, feelings, and first actions that tend to come before a relapse.
[Heading3] 6. A week does not protect you
You can still have a strong urge tomorrow.
You can still have a terrible day.
You may relapse during this course.
If that happens, do not ask whether the number went back to zero. Ask what you do in the next hour and the next morning.
It will be what you do next.
We will get to that.
[Heading3] 7. Next
Week 1 worked on the outside: your room, your phone, your nights, your time, the people around you.
Week 2 goes after something less visible.
Streaks. Relapse. Perfection. Shame. The stories you tell yourself after a bad day.
Those stories can turn one mistake into five.
Tomorrow, start with the number.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way.” – Marcus Aurelius
[Heading3] 8. Today's task
[TaskSummary] Reward yourself for finishing your first week.
Do something you enjoy because you finished Week 1. It does not have to be productive.
• Food: Get ice cream, a favourite snack, coffee, a meal you like, or cook something you normally save for a good day.
• Activity: Watch a film, play a game, go somewhere you like, take a long bath, visit a shop, read for pleasure, or spend time on a hobby.
• Social: Go for food with someone, meet a friend, play sport, or do something fun together.
• Money is tight: Use a free reward: a long walk, favourite film at home, game with friends, cooking something good, or an uninterrupted hour on something you enjoy.
The task is done when you have taken the reward. There is no extra recovery assignment today.
[Heading1] Week 2: Changing Your Mindset
[Heading2] Lesson 8: More Than a Streak
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“Not everything that can be counted counts, and not everything that counts can be counted.” – William Bruce Cameron
[Heading3] 2. The number helps
A streak is useful because it is simple.
Three days becomes four. Four becomes seven. You can look at one number and see that you kept a decision going for another day.
Keep the number if it helps.
Just do not confuse it with the whole job.
[Heading3] 3. Same number, different recovery
Imagine two people on Day 14.
The first has spent two weeks hiding from every trigger. He stays in bed when urges come, thinks about porn constantly, and gets through each day by trying not to break the streak.
The second still gets urges. But he sleeps better, sees friends, goes outside, catches the urge earlier, and knows what to do when it shows up.
Both screens say 14.
Those fourteen days do not mean the same thing.
[Heading3] 4. What are you actually trying to change?
A long streak can be encouraging, but the number is not the main result. The more important changes are what happens when an urge appears, how quickly you recover after a bad night, and whether porn is taking less time from the rest of your life.
It is less control by porn over what you do.
That includes the ten minutes before an urge. The urge itself. The morning after a bad night. Your sleep. Your room. The people you see. The speed with which you recover when something goes wrong.
A streak measures one outcome.
Recovery is larger than that.
[Heading3] 5. Zero can lie
This is where streaks become dangerous.
Thirty days becomes zero in one tap.
The screen makes it look as if thirty days of work disappeared. Then a second relapse can feel oddly cheap. The number is already gone. What is left to protect?
Everything you learned.
The thirty days still happened. The nights you handled well still happened. The room you changed is still changed. The skill you practised is still there.
A counter can reset.
You do not.
[Heading3] 6. Measure something else too
Besides your streak, what would you most like to improve?
How I respond to urges
How often I think about porn
My sleep and routine
My relationships and social life
How I feel about myself
Something else
Keep that answer.
It gives you another way to notice progress when the number tells too little.
[Heading3] 7. Keep the number small
Let a streak be a marker.
A long one can tell you something is working. A broken one can tell you something needs attention.
Neither is a verdict on your character.
It is a number.
Keep it that size.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“Measure what is measurable, and make measurable what is not so.” – Galileo Galilei
[Heading3] 8. Today's task
[TaskSummary] Notice three ways your behaviour has changed and repeat one today.
Open your Vici history or think back over the last seven days. Write down three things that have changed.
• Before or during an urge: Write about one thing you handle differently now, such as noticing it earlier, leaving the room, putting the phone down, or opening Urge Surfing.
• After a bad moment: Write about anything you do differently afterwards. If nothing has changed yet, say so.
• Outside porn: Write about anything that has improved this week, such as sleep, work, exercise, relationships, or a hobby.
Pick one improvement you wrote down and repeat that behaviour today. If nothing has improved yet, pick one behaviour you want to change and do it once today.
The task is done when the three changes are written and you have repeated one of them.
[Heading2] Lesson 9: After a Relapse
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“Our greatest glory is not in never falling, but in rising every time we fall.” – often attributed to Confucius
[Heading3] 2. The twenty minutes after
A relapse is one event.
What you tell yourself afterwards can affect the rest of the night or weekend.
I've ruined it.
I knew I couldn't do this.
Back to zero.
This always happens.
I may as well keep going.
The relapse might last twenty minutes.
That story can take the weekend.
[Heading3] 3. Serious is not catastrophic
Do not pretend a relapse is good.
If porn is causing problems and you chose to stop, going back matters. You should take it seriously.
But serious and catastrophic are different words for a reason.
A flat tyre can stop the car.
It does not require you to set the car on fire.
[Heading3] 4. Separate what happened from what you called yourself
The fact:
“I watched porn last night.”
The judgment:
“I have no discipline.”
Read them again.
The first sentence describes an event.
The second takes one event and turns it into a theory about your entire character.
That leap feels natural when you are ashamed.
It is still a leap.
[Heading3] 5. What arrives first?
Which thought is most likely to appear after you relapse?
“I've ruined my streak.” “I might as well do it again.” “I'm never going to change.” “I'm disgusted with myself.”
“I don't want to think about it.”
Something else
Do not argue with the thought yet.
Learn which one walks through the door first.
You cannot change the sequence if you never notice where it begins.
[Heading3] 6. The relapse does not own the next hour
One relapse does not decide whether you watch again ten minutes later.
It does not decide tomorrow morning.
It does not decide the week.
Those decisions have not happened.
In the moment, the mind bundles them together: I relapsed, therefore the night is gone.
It is not.
You still decide whether you close the tab, put the phone away, go to sleep, and continue the next morning as planned.
[Heading3] 7. Keep the event its real size
Later, an entire week of this course will deal with relapse and adversity.
For now, learn one distinction.
A relapse can show you which part of the sequence was still easy: staying up with the phone, being alone after an argument, opening a certain account, or continuing after the first search.
It cannot tell you that everything failed.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“A gem cannot be polished without friction, nor a man perfected without trials.” – Seneca
[Heading3] 8. Today's task
[TaskSummary] Review what happened after your last relapse and plan how to stop the next part sooner.
Open Notes. Think about your most recent relapse. If you have never relapsed, use the closest call you remember.
• What happened next: Write what you did during the first 30 minutes afterwards. Be literal: kept watching, stayed in bed, kept scrolling, skipped sleep, cancelled plans, or whatever happened.
• First chance to stop: Write the earliest point when you could have stopped the next part from happening.
• Next time: Write three actions you can start within five minutes. For example: close every porn tab, put the phone in the kitchen, take a shower, then return to breakfast, work, class, or sleep.

[Heading3] 2. The lie inside a strong urge

[Heading2] Lesson 36: What is Willpower?
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“Energy and persistence conquer all things.” – Benjamin Franklin
[Heading3] 2. What willpower is
Willpower is the effort of following a decision when another option feels more attractive right now. You have decided to work, sleep, stop scrolling, or leave the room, but part of you wants the easier or more immediately rewarding option. Willpower is what you use to keep the chosen action in place.
That is why it appears far outside porn recovery. You use it when you get out of bed instead of snoozing again, finish a page instead of checking your phone, or stop a game when you said you would. It is not a special personality trait that some people simply have and others do not.
[Heading3] 3. What willpower does
In a difficult moment, willpower does a few ordinary jobs. It keeps the reason for your earlier decision in view, stops or delays the first impulse, and gets you to carry out a different action even when you do not feel like doing it.
With porn, that may mean closing the search, putting the phone down, standing up, and leaving the room while part of you still wants to continue. The urge does not have to disappear before you act.
[Heading3] 4. Why willpower feels harder sometimes
Willpower is not equally easy to use at every moment. A decision that feels obvious at 3 p.m. can take much more effort at 1 a.m. after two hours of scrolling. Tiredness, stress, sexual arousal, alcohol, and easy access to porn can all make the immediate option more attractive.
That does not mean a fixed tank of self-control has emptied. It means the choice has become harder because the circumstances changed.
[Heading3] 5. Do not make willpower do every job
Willpower matters most when the conflict cannot be removed. But you should not create the same difficult choice every night just to prove you can win it.
If porn usually begins with your phone in bed, move the phone before bedtime. If private scrolling after midnight is risky, decide when it ends earlier in the evening. If Vici's Urge Surfing tool helps you interrupt a search, keep the shortcut somewhere you can reach in one tap. Use willpower when you need it, but make the avoidable decisions easier beforehand.

[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“The only way to have a friend is to be one.” – Ralph Waldo Emerson
[Heading3] 2. Friendship is usually repetitive
Most good friendships do not begin with one unforgettable conversation. They begin with another ordinary one: sitting near the same person, training together, eating after class, sending another message, or remembering the thing they mentioned last week.
[Heading3] 3. Stop waiting for instant chemistry
Sometimes you meet somebody and know immediately. Fine. Most relationships are less cinematic. They grow through fairly ordinary contact between people who keep appearing in the same place. The important word is not “chemistry.” It is “keep.” Keep showing up, keep talking, or make the second move.
[Heading3] 4. Put yourself where repetition is built in
Friendship is easier in places where the same people keep returning: a sport, a class, a society, volunteering, workplace lunch, a weekly game, a religious community, or even a cafe where familiar faces appear. You no longer have to create social contact from zero every week. The structure does some of the work.
[Heading3] 5. Where can something grow?
Which place in your life has the best chance of producing repeated contact?
Work, school or university
Sport or exercise
A club, society or hobby
A faith or community group
Friends of existing friends
I need to find somewhere new
Choose the realistic one. You need a place you will return to. Not a social life that looks good in theory.
[Heading3] 6. The second move matters
The first conversation creates an acquaintance. The follow-up gives the relationship a chance. Ask whether they are going next week or send the article you mentioned. Suggest lunch after training. Sit beside them again. Do not wait for the other person to perform every continuation. Small follow-ups keep the relationship from resetting to zero.
[Heading3] 7. Do not rush familiarity
Some people will stay acquaintances. Some friendships will fade. A few will deepen so gradually that you cannot identify the day it happened. You do not control which one becomes important. You can control whether you are there often enough for it to happen.
Show up again.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“Friendship is a sheltering tree.” – Samuel Taylor Coleridge

[Heading2] Lesson 62: Unhealthy Relationships
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“Associate with people who are likely to improve you.” – Seneca
[Heading3] 2. More connection is not always better
This week has pushed you toward people. Do not turn that into: “Every relationship deserves more of me.” some people regularly leave you more anxious, smaller, ashamed, and pulled toward things you are trying to leave. Connected is not the same as well connected.
Quality still matters.
[Heading3] 3. Judge the pattern, not one bad afternoon
Everyone gets selfish, says the wrong thing, misses a call, or has a bad mood. Do not judge a relationship from one argument. Look at what happens repeatedly. Do you usually feel respected? Can you disagree without being punished? Can you say no without the other person ignoring it? Can you be honest without constantly trying to prevent their reaction?
Those questions tell you more.
[Heading3] 4. Social environments train behaviour too
If people around you mock every attempt to change, pressure you back into habits you are leaving, or become hostile when you set a boundary, spending more time with them can make recovery harder. You do not need everyone to approve. You do need relationships where changing your behaviour is allowed.
Notice that.

This is not an instruction to cut somebody off after one quiz. It is a reason to look carefully.
[Heading3] 6. Boundaries do not need a speech
Spend less time there or stop taking every late-night call. Decline the invitation. Refuse the joke at your expense. Keep one subject private or leave when an interaction becomes cruel. You do not need a speech. Change what you actually allow the other person to ask of you or say to you.
[Heading3] 7. Loneliness lowers standards if you let it
Loneliness can make bad company look better than no company. Keep the standard simple: the relationship should not repeatedly leave you afraid, humiliated, pressured, or smaller than you were before the interaction.
Choose people with care.

[Heading2] Lesson 63: Healthy Relationships
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“Friendship is a single soul dwelling in two bodies.” – Aristotle, traditionally attributed
[Heading3] 2. Good does not mean easy
A good relationship still contains conflict. People disappoint each other, misunderstand things, get tired, and sometimes become selfish. The difference is that conflict does not destroy the basic sense that both people are still on the same side. You can apologise, repair, and keep going.
[Heading3] 3. You can stop performing
A healthy relationship asks less acting from you. You do not need to be funny every time. Successful every time. Fine every time. You can say: “This week has been rough.” And leave the sentence there. Being known is one of the things a screen cannot imitate no matter how much content it gives you.
[Heading3] 4. Support is reciprocal
A good relationship is not another service you consume. It asks something back: remember what matters to the other person, listen, show up, accept some inconvenience, and care about what happened in their day. That is part of why real connection feels different from stimulation on a screen.

[Heading3] 6. Healthy relationships need maintenance too
People often spend huge amounts of energy repairing bad relationships while the healthy ones receive whatever time is left. Reverse that sometimes. Answer the message, make the plan, say thank you, ask the second question, or remember the birthday. Good relationships also need maintenance.
[Heading3] 7. Recovery should not stay private
It is easy to turn porn recovery into a project that happens entirely inside your own head: triggers, thoughts, rules, and streaks.

[Heading2] Lesson 64: Trauma
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“The child is father of the man.” – William Wordsworth
[Heading3] 2. Old needs do not always disappear
Trauma is not a hidden explanation that every person with a porn problem needs to uncover. Some people have lived through events that still affect how safe they feel, how they respond to conflict or intimacy, or how quickly their body reacts to reminders. Other people do not have a trauma story that belongs in their recovery.
Do not go looking for one because the lesson has the word in its title.
[Heading3] 3. What worked then
When a genuinely threatening or overwhelming experience is over, some responses can remain useful for too long. You may stay alert for danger, avoid certain situations, go numb, withdraw, feel shame quickly, or find closeness difficult even when the present situation is safer.
Some studies find associations between trauma histories, post-traumatic stress symptoms, and compulsive sexual behaviour. That does not mean trauma causes every porn problem, and it does not mean porn proves that trauma happened.
[Heading3] 4. Do not invent a childhood explanation
Work only with experiences you already know still affect you. You do not need to recover a memory, decide whether your childhood was “bad enough,” or connect every urge to the past. If nothing specific comes to mind, leave the past alone. Work with the present behaviour you can actually describe instead of inventing a trauma explanation.
[Heading3] 5. What did you need more of?
If there is a past experience you already know still affects you, where does it show up now?
Conflict or criticism makes my body react strongly
Rejection or abandonment is hard to tolerate
Sex, touch, or intimacy can bring up fear, shame, or numbness
I shut down or feel detached when stressed
I avoid certain people, places, memories, or situations
I use porn, scrolling, fantasy, or something else to go numb
I do not know of a past experience that is affecting me in this way now
Choose only what you recognise. You do not need a painful answer.
[Heading3] 6. Keep the focus on what happens now
An app cannot treat trauma. This lesson can only help you notice whether a known past experience still shows up in present behaviour and whether that is something you want professional help with. If a known trauma response is interfering with daily life — for example frequent intrusive memories, nightmares, panic, dissociation, strong avoidance, or difficulty feeling safe — professional support from someone trained in trauma can be useful. You do not have to retell the event in detail here.
[Heading3] 7. Keep the parts that still work
Trauma work is not a hunt for damage. People can carry strengths as well as difficulties out of hard experiences: independence, humour, caution, persistence, sensitivity to other people, or a strong sense of responsibility. Keep what still helps. Get support for what still hurts.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“It is never too late to be what you might have been.” – George Eliot, often attributed
[Heading3] 8. Today's task
[TaskSummary] Write about a past experience that still affects you and how you want to handle it next time.
Write briefly about a past experience you already know still affects you, what happens when it comes up now, what you usually do to get away from it, and what you could do next time.
• Past: Write about the experience only if one already comes to mind. If not, note that you do not know of a trauma link here.
• Now: Write what happens when it comes up, such as tension, panic, numbness, shame, withdrawing, avoiding intimacy, or another reaction you recognise.
• Escape: Write what you usually do to get away from the feeling or memory. Porn can be one answer, but so can scrolling, drinking, isolating, overworking, or shutting down.
• Next time: Write what you could do instead, such as leaving an unsafe setting, grounding yourself in the room, calling a trusted person, writing down what is happening, or contacting a professional.
Prepare for that situation today. Save the contact, book the appointment, write down grounding steps, or tell the trusted person you may reach out. If you do not know of a trauma link, leave it there; do not invent one.
The task is done when you have written about all four parts and prepared anything you may need next time.

Maybe work drains you until you want to switch your mind off. Maybe a relationship means waiting for the next argument. Then you leave. The evening starts.
And escape suddenly looks more attractive. That connection is worth noticing.

“Just leave” is cheap advice when somebody else has to live with the consequences. Money matters. So do family, housing, work, immigration, school and safety. Separate the questions. [Heading3] 7. Endurance is not always the goal
There are times when endurance is necessary. There are also times when “being strong” becomes a reason to stay somewhere that keeps hurting you. Do not confuse the two. If a place repeatedly pulls you apart, changing your relationship with it may be the stronger move.

[Heading2] Lesson 66: Self-Criticism
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“No one can make you feel inferior without your consent.” – Eleanor Roosevelt
[Heading3] 2. Listen to the first sentence
You make a mistake and the commentary starts: idiot, weak, pathetic, of course you did it again. Because the voice sounds strict, it can pass for accountability.
[Heading3] 3. Useful criticism points somewhere
Useful feedback is specific. “You stayed up until two with your phone in bed.” There is something to change in that sentence. “You have no discipline.” There is not. One names the behaviour. The other turns the behaviour into a verdict about the person.
[Heading3] 4. Anger can feel productive
Being furious with yourself can create a burst of energy. You delete everything, make ten rules, promise it was the last time, and decide to start again harder. For a few hours that can feel like progress, but the anger eventually runs out. You still have to follow the plan on an ordinary day when the emotion is gone.
[Heading3] 5. What does your voice sound like?
After a relapse or mistake, what do you usually sound like inside your own head?
Angry and insulting
Disappointed but practical
Hopeless
Anxious and obsessive
I avoid thinking about it
It varies
You do not need an endlessly positive inner voice. You need one that can describe a mistake without exaggerating it and tell you what to do next.
[Heading3] 6. Keep the information
Take a sentence you use against yourself. Strip out the insult. “I am pathetic” can become “I keep relapsing when I stay up alone.” “I ruin everything” can become “I had one bad night, and I need to stop it carrying into today.” The second sentence is not nicer for the sake of being nice.
It is more accurate. And accuracy gives you somewhere to act.
[Heading3] 7. Criticise the decision
You are allowed to dislike what you did. You are allowed to be disappointed. But if every mistake becomes an attack on your worth, you lose sight of the thing that actually needs changing. Criticise the decision when it deserves criticism.
Leave the person intact.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“The greatest discovery of my generation is that a human being can alter his life by altering his attitudes.” – William James, commonly attributed

[Heading2] Lesson 67: Self-Loathing
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“Hatred is blind.” – Oscar Wilde
[Heading3] 2. The sentence gets bigger
Self-criticism says, “I did something bad.” Self-loathing says, “There is something bad about me.” That extra step changes the problem. If the whole person is defective, why would fixing tonight matter? Why would one good decision count? The behaviour becomes a biography.
[Heading3] 3. Secrecy makes it worse
Porn problems are often private. You clear the history, hide the screen, make promises nobody hears, and carry the embarrassment alone. That secrecy can make the habit feel larger and stranger than it is, and it can make you assume other people would judge you as harshly as you judge yourself.
You usually do not know that.
[Heading3] 4. Your worth is not the behaviour
You can take repeated behaviour seriously without turning it into a judgment about your whole worth. A person can lie and later become more honest, hurt somebody and apologise, or waste years and then use the next year differently. The behaviour matters, but it does not describe everything about the person.
[Heading3] 5. Which thought lands hardest?
Which thought shows up most often when you feel ashamed?
“I'm disgusting”
“I'm weak”
“Nobody would respect me if they knew”
“I've wasted too much time”
“I will never change”
Something else
I do not usually feel this way
Look closely at the sentence. Does it describe something that happened? Or does it claim to explain everything you are?
[Heading3] 6. Make the claim smaller
If the thought is global, narrow it until it becomes accurate. “I am weak” becomes “I have struggled to control this behaviour in certain situations.” “I've wasted my life” becomes “I have spent more time on this than I wanted.” Neither sentence has to feel good.
It just has to be true.
[Heading3] 7. Let new evidence count
You have spent weeks making different choices. That does not prove you will never struggle again. It proves something smaller and more important: “I can never change” is already too broad. Do not tell a story that refuses to admit new evidence.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“Nothing is more deceitful than the appearance of humility. It is often only carelessness of opinion, and sometimes an indirect boast.” – Jane Austen

[Heading2] Lesson 68: Self-Compassion
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote
“Be kind, for everyone you meet is fighting a hard battle.” – Often attributed to Plato
[Heading3] 2. You are included
Imagine a close friend told you they had relapsed. Or failed an exam. Lost a job. Got rejected. Had a week they were ashamed of. You probably would not tell them that one bad event proved they were worthless. Then the person becomes you, and the standard changes.
Self-compassion begins by refusing that exception.
[Heading3] 3. Compassion is not permission
Being compassionate does not mean saying everything is fine. Sometimes it was a bad decision. Sometimes something needs to change. You can say both without adding three days of punishment. Accountability asks what happened and what comes next. Punishment adds pain because you hope pain will somehow teach the lesson.
[Heading3] 4. Shame can make it harder to learn from a mistake
There will be urges, rough weeks, and possibly relapses. If every mistake turns into hours of insulting yourself, you are less likely to look clearly at what happened. Calm down first, then work out what made the relapse easier and what you will change next time.
Then you can act.
[Heading3] 5. What would you say to someone else?
Imagine a close friend described the exact problem you are hardest on yourself about. What would you most likely say first?
“One mistake doesn't erase everything”
“Let's figure out what happened”
“You need to change something, but beating yourself up won't help”
“Get through today first”
Something else
That voice is not soft because it ignores the problem. It is useful because it stays with the problem.
[Heading3] 6. Keep the language ordinary
You do not need affirmations. You do not need to speak to yourself like a therapist. Try: “I'm disappointed, but I can deal with this.” “That was a bad decision. I know what made it easier.” “I've had a hard week. Tonight needs to be simple.” Nothing grand.
Just enough kindness to keep working.
[Heading3] 7. Work with yourself
You are the person who has to carry out every change in this course. That makes your relationship with yourself practical, not sentimental. Demand better when you need to. Then help yourself do it.
[Heading3] Closing quote
“If you want others to be happy, practice compassion. If you want to be happy, practice compassion.” – Dalai Lama

[Heading2] Lesson 82: Lessons From Addiction Recovery
[Heading3] 1. Opening quote

[Heading3] 2. Do not stretch the comparison too far
Problematic pornography use is not the same thing as alcohol, nicotine, opioids or gambling. The risks differ. The treatment needs differ. If porn starts becoming harder to control again, use more support instead of waiting for the problem to become severe.

Another lesson from addiction care is simple. People do not always solve recurring problems alone. Depending on the problem, therapy, support groups, medical care and trusted relationships can all have a place. If porn use keeps causing serious impairment and self-guided attempts are not enough, getting professional help is a sensible escalation.
It is not evidence that you lacked discipline.
